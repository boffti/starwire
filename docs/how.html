Where repos come from

A repo lands on starwire when someone points at it. We collect four kinds of picks:

  • Developer curators who post open source projects for their followers. We read the repo link from each post.
  • Hacker News stories that link to a GitHub repo and reach 30 points, or 20 for Show HN.
  • GitHub Trending, the weekly list.
  • Reader submissions through the submission form. Your own projects are welcome; the form asks you to say so.

Each of these counts as a separate source. A repo picked by several of them is shown as picked 2×, picked 3× and so on.

What gets listed

  • The repo must be public on GitHub and not archived.
  • Submissions need at least 10 stars. Every submission gets a reply on its issue with the result.
  • New repos are reviewed on each update. Anything that looks like spam, malware or isn't open source software is hidden.
  • A repo that is deleted, made private or archived drops off on the next refresh.

Listing a repo isn't an endorsement or a security review. Read the code and the license before you run anything.

Categories and descriptions

Each new repo is put in one of 15 categories and gets a one-line description, written by an AI model (Claude) from the repo's own description and README, following fixed rules: say what it does in plain words, no hype. Submitters can suggest a category. These are the parts most likely to be wrong, so if one is, open an issue with the repo link and we'll fix it.

How Trending works

Trending shows repos gaining stars fastest right now for their size. It uses only stars we measured ourselves.

1. Stars gained in the last 7 days

Every day we record each repo's star count. The gain is today's count minus the count from 7 days ago.

  • A repo created less than 7 days ago gained all its stars this week, so its gain is its star count.
  • While we have fewer than 7 days of counts for a repo, it shows only the days measured, as +255 in 1 day. Nothing is projected forward.
  • For a repo under 30 days old, the missing days are filled with its own average since it was created. That's an estimate, so it's marked with a ~, as in ~+700 a week.

2. Adjusted for size

A repo with 200,000 stars picks up hundreds a week out of habit. A small one gaining the same is far more remarkable. So the ranking divides the gain by the square root of the repo's size:

heat = stars gained in 7 days / √(stars + 100)

The square root keeps big repos in the running without letting them drown out small ones, and the + 100 stops a brand new repo with a handful of stars from jumping to the top on its first few. A repo needs at least 50 stars in the week to appear in Trending at all.

Example: +2,000 stars on a 3,000-star repo scores 2,000 / √3,100 ≈ 36. The same +2,000 on a 100,000-star repo scores about 6.

What never affects ranking

  • No one can pay to be listed or ranked higher.
  • The StarDash card in the list and in repo panels is our own project, made by the same person who makes starwire. It's always labelled and never changes the order.
  • There are no accounts and no ads. Your saves stay in your browser.
  • Visit counts (below) don't change the ranking. If they ever do, it will be listed under changes first.
  • The README previews load from GitHub, and the two libraries that render them load from cdnjs with integrity checks, so a tampered copy is refused.

Some signs are left out instead of judged: no releases (many projects never use GitHub releases), fewer than 5 issues opened and closed in 90 days, or a repo with no measured star gain. "How it compares" puts the same numbers next to repos that replace the same tools, or its closest neighbours in its subarea.

What we count

starwire counts visits with GoatCounter to learn what's useful: no cookies, nothing stored in your browser, no IP addresses kept, no way to follow you to other sites. The numbers are public, so you see exactly what we see.

  • Page visits, without the link's settings or saved lists.
  • Searches, once you stop typing, and whether anything matched. This shows where search falls short.
  • The view or area you open, repos you open or save, and clicks through to GitHub. This shows which repos people actually look at.

If your browser sends Do Not Track or Global Privacy Control, nothing is counted and the counting script isn't loaded at all.

Known limits

  • Daily star counts started on November 6, 2026. Until about November 13, many repos show fewer than 7 days of gains.
  • Bought or fake stars can't be detected reliably from public data yet. Since mid-2026 GitHub no longer shows who starred a repo, and the largest study of fake stars (He et al., ICSE 2026) found that even good detectors can wrongly flag honest projects. A sudden jump or few forks isn't proof either: a Hacker News launch looks the same. We're researching this internally, and nothing on starwire is ranked, hidden or labelled because of it. If you believe a listed repo bought its stars, tell us.
  • Picks come from what curators and readers find, so some corners of open source are better covered than others. Submissions help fill the gaps.
